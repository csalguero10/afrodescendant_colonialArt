ABOUT THE EXHIBITION

This virtual exhibition explores how Africans and their descendants were represented in colonial American art and the spaces they came to occupy in viceregal society. Their presence in colonial painting was far from marginal. Yet the images that portray them are not faithful records of reality but constructions shaped by the ideals and anxieties of those who commissioned and produced them. Casta paintings in particular celebrated mestizaje while reinforcing a hierarchy based on skin color and calidad. Archival sources, by contrast, reveal enslaved and free people working in households, markets, and workshops, joining guilds, militias, and confraternities, and in exceptional cases achieving recognition.

Through four sections (casta paintings, secular scenes, religious images, and portraits) and two routes, one general and one specialized, the exhibition invites visitors to look critically at these works.It asks them to recognize both the colonial gaze that produced the works and the presence of those who, within an order shaped by slavery, negotiated their place in the viceregal world. The exhibition seeks to analyze the representation of people of African descent across genres of colonial painting and to contrast the idealized image the works project with the reality recorded in archival sources. It aims to make visible the spaces this population occupied in labor, guilds, militias, and confraternities, while keeping in view that slavery was the dominant condition and mobility the exception. It also examines how clothing, gender, and composition express both colonial control and forms of negotiation and resistance.
In the Spanish American colonies, Africans and their descendants were often denied individuation in representation and were instead depicted as generic types or as members of a group.1 As Jaime Borja notes in Los ingenios del pincel, "of Black people—mostly enslaved, but also free men who acted as settlers and even conquistadors—no visual memory remains,"2 despite their constant presence in colonial society. These groups remained marginally represented in painting as a reflection of their exclusion from access to the forms of power that guaranteed visibility. In this sense, portraits and colonial painting did not operate merely as representational tools, but as exercises of power, capable of determining who deserved to be seen and how.

Nonetheless, colonial paintings include figures (particularly in the middle and background) whose inclusion itself demonstrates the social fluidity of a world shaped by movement, settlement, and relocation. Historically, figures of Africans and their descendants in Latin American colonial art have received limited interpretive attention. This marginalization extends beyond mere compositional hierarchy, reflecting broader assumptions that naturalized slavery as the expected condition of Black individuals in colonial society. Recent scholarship, however, has begun to challenge these views, highlighting the diverse roles of Black people, from military or commercial positions to artistic production,3 and stressing the need to reconsider their visibility and significance within colonial visual culture.
