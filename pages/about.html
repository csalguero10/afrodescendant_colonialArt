ABOUT THE EXHIBITION

This virtual exhibition brings together works produced in the American viceroyalties to examine how Africans and their descendants were represented in colonial art, and what places they came to occupy in the society of their time. Their presence in viceregal painting was far from marginal. They appear in casta paintings, on folding screens and in secular scenes, in religious iconography, and in portraiture. Yet these images are not faithful reflections of reality. They are constructions that condense the values, fears, and aspirations of those who commissioned and produced them. Casta painting in particular celebrated mestizaje while legitimizing a hierarchy in which skin color, purity of blood (limpieza de sangre), and calidad were meant to fix each person’s place within the colonial order. Against this idealized order, documentary sources reveal a more complex society.

In it, slavery was the normative condition of people of African descent. Even so, enslaved and free men and women took part in domestic and urban labor, in guilds, in the militias of pardos and morenos, and in confraternities devoted to saints such as Saint Benedict of Palermo. Some, like the mulatto painter Juan Correa, attained positions of recognition. Such mobility was exceptional, hard-won, and closely watched by the authorities, who issued ordinances to restrict the autonomy of free Black and mulatto people. The exhibition is organized into four sections: casta paintings, secular scenes, religious images, and portraits. It offers two routes, one for a general audience and one for a specialized audience. It invites visitors to read these works critically, recognizing in their clothing, gestures, and composition both the colonial gaze that produced them and the traces of those who, within its limits, negotiated their place in the viceregal world.

To analyze how Africans and their descendants were represented in colonial American art, and which spaces they came to occupy in viceregal society. The exhibition contrasts the images with the documentary sources of their time.

Specific objectives

  • To show the presence of people of African descent across pictorial genres: casta paintings, secular scenes, religious images, and portraits.
  • To examine casta painting as an ideological instrument that naturalized a hierarchy based on skin color, purity of blood, and calidad.
  • To distinguish between the idealized image the works project and the social reality recorded in archives, chronicles, and censuses.
  • To make visible the spaces occupied by people of African descent: domestic and urban labor, guilds, the militias of pardos and morenos, and confraternities. The exhibition stresses that slavery was the normative condition and that mobility was exceptional and closely watched.
  • To analyze clothing, jewelry, gesture, and composition as sites where calidad, colonial control, and forms of negotiation and resistance intersect.
  • To explore the intersection of race and gender, particularly in the representation of women of African descent.
  • To offer two routes, one for a general audience and one for a specialized audience, each providing a critical reading accessible to its readers.
From the sixteenth century onward, the transatlantic slave trade forcibly brought thousands of Africans to the American viceroyalties through ports such as Veracruz and Cartagena de Indias. They were put to work in mines, haciendas, textile workshops, convents, and urban households. Their unions with Spaniards and Indigenous people gave rise to a diverse population that colonial authorities sought to classify through categories such as mulato, pardo, or zambo. In the eighteenth century, Enlightenment thought and the Bourbon reforms intensified the drive to order and rank society. In this context casta painting emerged, while people of African descent, both enslaved and free, continued to negotiate their place within an order that sought to fix them at its margins.
The hierarchies these images helped construct did not disappear with the end of the colonial period. The association between skin color, origin, and social worth persists in forms of discrimination that still affect Afro-descendant populations across Latin America. For a long time, national narratives minimized or erased their presence in history, and only in recent decades have countries such as Colombia and Mexico granted constitutional recognition to their Afro-descendant peoples. Returning to these works allows us to recognize the so-called “third root” as a constitutive part of American societies. It also helps us understand how images take part in shaping identities and inequalities, and to question the ways of seeing that still shape how we represent others.
The exhibition draws on the iconographic and iconological method. Each work is first analyzed in its formal elements (composition, scale, placement of figures, gesture, clothing, and accessories) and then in its historical and ideological context. This analysis is set against period sources, including the chronicles of Thomas Gage, Gemelli Carreri, and Francisco de Ajofrín, the Recopilación de Leyes de Indias, archival documents, and censuses, as well as recent scholarship on people of African descent. This cross-reading makes it possible to distinguish between what the images project and what the sources record, without treating the works as neutral testimony. The texts are organized into two levels of reading: an accessible one for a general audience and a more analytically detailed one for a specialized audience.
