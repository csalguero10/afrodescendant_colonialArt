ECHOES OF HERITAGE

The colonial past is not behind glass; it lives in our skin, in our music, in how we see each other, and in how our societies are structured today.

Adoration
and Majesty

Religious Painting Hall

The Artwork

Saint Dominic and the
Counts of Casa Bayona

The Count of Casa Bayona commissioned this painting to record his household's devotion to Saint Dominic, haloed and elevated at the center, teaching with one hand raised. Around him, two groups gather: the Bayona family in the foreground, clergy and nobility behind.

Among the family, one Afro-descendant figure sits apart from the rest — present in the same sacred scene, but set off from it by posture and dress.

Reflection

Present, But
Set Apart

Art historians still argue about what we're looking at. Some read this figure's seated posture as deference — a convention used to depict religious instruction. Others point out he isn't kneeling at all: spine straight, gaze steady, closer to an attentive witness than a subordinate.

Even the painting's old museum catalog labels him "esclavo" — enslaved — a word the image itself never actually confirms.

It's colonial Havana's social fabric in miniature: a Dominican religious order, a titled Creole family, and an Afro-descendant member of that same household — one interwoven world, not two separate ones.

Today

Unnamed,
Yet Present

He has no name in this painting — no cartouche records him the way one records Saint Dominic or the Bayona family. Think about the present: how often is someone present in a room, a classroom, a family photo, yet still read as peripheral to it?

Being included in the frame has never automatically meant being seen as an equal within it — a question this painting raises just as sharply today.

The Market,
the Street, Life

Secular Scenes

The Artwork

A Family,
and a Legal Category

This is a family portrait, and it's also a legal classification. The inscription names it directly: "De Negro, y de India, China Cambuja." By colonial law, the child of a Black father and an Indigenous mother was registered under this specific casta label — one of dozens the system used to catalog mestizaje.

Miguel Cabrera, one of the most sought-after painters in New Spain, built much of his career painting combinations exactly like this one.

Clothing

Clothing as a
Social Document

Look at the fruit at their feet, painted almost like a scientific catalog — prickly pears, bananas, sapodillas, peaches, jicamas, plums, cherimoyas. The same classifying eye is pointed at the family above it.

The father wears a redingote, a riding coat associated at the time with servants and military dress — he may well have worked as a coachman, a common trade for Afro-descendant men in Mexico City. The mother is dressed as a woman of means: a finely woven huipil, an embroidered rebozo, gold earrings and a necklace. And look at the daughter's skin: the painter made it visibly lighter than her father's — mixing, made legible on canvas.

Today

We Dance
Resistance

That same tension — a label imposed from outside versus an identity built from within — still shapes how Latin America talks about race and class today. When you dance cumbia in Colombia, festejo in Peru, samba in Brazil, or son in Cuba, you are dancing resistance.

Our Latin American identity is incomprehensible without the cultural, rhythmic, and culinary revolution that these people planted on our continent, against all odds.
03 / 04

The Canvas
of the System

Casta Painting

The Artwork

A Catalog
of Inequality

Stand in front of this painting. It looks like a catalog, doesn't it? That's exactly what it was meant to be. The title spells out the formula: a Creole Black woman from Guinea, a Spanish man — "producen mulato."

"Mulatto," "Zambo," "Morisco." The Spanish Empire invented the casta system to classify mestizaje itself, building a pyramid where the whiter your skin was, the more rights and status you had.

Visible Pride

A Law,
and Its Exception

Colonial law generally forbade Black and mulatta women, enslaved or free, from wearing gold, pearls, or silk. But it made one exception: a free Black or mulatta woman married to a Spaniard could wear gold earrings set with pearls, a necklace, and a little velvet trim.

Her partner here is Spanish — exactly that exception. Her silk dress with its blue-and-white floral embroidery, her raised hand gesturing toward him, the child beside visible coins: every detail was there to document wealth and fix a genealogy, not simply to decorate a canvas.

The Harshest Mirror

Colorism
Still at Work

The legal casta system disappeared with independence, but colorism and structural racism remained.

When statistics show that skin tone still influences a person's economic opportunities, we are seeing the ghost of these paintings at work in the 21st century.

This is no coincidence. It was a system designed three hundred years ago to work exactly this way.

04 / 04

The Leader,
Freedom

Portrait & Identity

The Artwork

Three Names,
Painted with Dignity

We end our tour facing three men named directly in the painting: Don Francisco de Arobe and his sons, Pedro and Domingo. They led the cimarrones — self-liberated Africans and their descendants — who built an autonomous community on the Esmeraldas coast, in present-day Ecuador, after a shipwreck decades earlier freed a group of enslaved Africans onto that shore.

In 1599, they negotiated vassalage with the Spanish Crown on their own terms — and this portrait was sent across the ocean to Madrid to record that pact.

Legacy

Three Worlds,
One Canvas

Look at what they hold and wear: African spears, carried the way weapons of authority are carried; jewelry and ornament drawn from Andean indigenous dress; and the fine European doublets, lace collars, and pearls of formal colonial portraiture. Three worlds, painted onto three bodies at once — this is what transculturation looks like.

Recognizing and celebrating our Afro-descendant roots is not an act of historical nostalgia. It is an act of justice — and a way of unlearning the colonial prejudices we still carry. We carry their legacy in our voice.
End of the Tour

You have moved through four rooms, four artworks, four moments of encounter between colonial history and our society today. These paintings are mirrors. What you recognize in them is not just art: it is the invisible architecture of our present.
