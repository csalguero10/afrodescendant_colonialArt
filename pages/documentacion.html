PROJECT CONTEXT

Afrodescendant Colonial Art is the final project for the course Information Modelling and Web Technologies, held by Prof. Fabio Vitali, within the Master's program in Digital Humanities and Digital Knowledge (DHDK).

The choice of this narrative is connected to my research interests. It builds on my ongoing thesis research on how Afro-descendant people were represented in Latin American colonial art. The final research is more complex, as it explores AI bias when analyzing colonial paintings. However, for this project, the aim is to present 15 images that show the complexity of the representation of Afro-descendant people in colonial art. Within this small corpus, we can see that even though the representation of Afro-descendant people was reduced to the condition of enslavement, the historical record shows a far more complex reality: free and enslaved Black men and women working, living their lives, and participating in the social, economic, and religious life of the colonies.

Colonial art is, in many ways, often misunderstood. Seen from a distance and without context, it is often dismissed as dull, or reduced to its ties with Catholic religious imagery (a direct consequence of the evangelizing project that accompanied Spanish colonization). Yet this subject is considerably more complex, as colonial art is one of the clearest visual records of the transculturation and mestizaje between the groups that shaped colonial society — Spaniards, Africans and their descendants, and the Indigenous peoples who inhabited these territories long before the Spanish arrival. Reading these images carefully reveals the foundations of the cultural identities we still recognize today in Latin America.

From this premise, the exhibition for this project was organized into two narrative routes. "Did You Know?", aimed at a general audience, uses cultural identity as an entry point: many of the traditions, aesthetics, and social behaviors of our present in Latin America have roots in this period, even when we do not always recognize them. "Into the Roots", aimed at a more specialized audience, offers a deeper, more academic reading of the same works, with closer attention to iconographic and historiographic analysis.

UX AND UI DESIGN

As I mentioned before, this exhibition focuses on the representation of Afro-descendant people in Latin American colonial art. Based on this, I decided to explore different platforms, websites, and applications to understand how I could create a digital exhibition that would be accessible to different audiences, keep their attention, and prevent them from abandoning the routes.

Fifteen images were selected for the exhibition from a dataset I have been building independently of this course, now comprising 130 works, assembled to represent the range of ways Afro-descendant people were depicted in colonial painting, from religious scenes to casta paintings, secular scenes, and portraits which are the thematic chosen for this exhibition.

As I will explain in the next section, the color palette was extracted from real colonial paintings, while the gold was chosen to evoke their gilded frames. The typography was chosen to create a clear hierarchy and facilitate reading, with a condensed display face for titles, a humanist sans for body text, and a monospace face for small labels. Since studying Art History, I have realized that a black background is the best option for highlighting the colors of the paintings and avoiding distractions.

That reading was then translated into a concrete information model. I designed a quick information architecture to understand how the website's information would be distributed, establishing a hierarchy of pages (home -> about, collection, etc.), the sections of each page, a structured record for each artwork, and a set of iconographic "details" — close-up zooms — pointing to the specific elements, a gesture, a garment, an object, that carry the most interpretive weight. After that, creating the HTML skeleton was really easy.

User Archetypes

Before defining the two narrative routes, two fictional archetypes were sketched out to represent the exhibition's audience extremes: one with no prior interest in the subject, one academically trained but outside the field.

Valentina Ruiz, 23

General Public — "Did You Know?" route

Communications graduate working in social media marketing in Bogotá. She has no background in art history and rarely visits museums; she lands on Afrodescendant Colonial Art after a friend shares the link, out of curiosity more than any real interest in colonial painting.

Goals

Something she can skim on her phone in a few minutes; a quick, personal reason to care about colonial art today.

Frustrations

Long academic texts, unfamiliar terms (mulato, casta, peniquia, etc.), and walls of text with no visual hook.

"If it doesn't grab me in the first ten seconds, I close the tab."

Prof. Andrés Molina, 47

Specialist — "Into the Roots" route

Associate professor of economic history, specializing in 19th-century trade and labor — not colonial art or Afro-descendant studies. A colleague points him to Afrodescendant Colonial Art, and he is curious whether visual sources could complement his own research, despite having no training in iconographic analysis.

Goals

Rigorous sourcing, a legible methodology, and enough depth to cite or discuss the material with students and colleagues.

Frustrations

Pop-history oversimplification, uncited claims, and content that assumes an art-historical expertise he doesn't have.

"I need to know where this claim comes from before I can use it."

Key Reference for Interactivity

The Second Canvas platform built by the Spanish studio Mad Pixel Factory and used by both the Mauritshuis and SMK (Statens Museum for Kunst, Denmark), was the clearest reference for how to present high-resolution images interactively. Its deep-zoom viewer and guided iconographic "details" directly inspired the zoom-and-pan viewer and detail points used throughout this exhibition's collection.

Design Decisions

With those references in hand, a small UI kit was defined for the exhibition — a dark, gold-accented palette evoking oil varnish and gilded frames without competing with the artworks, a black background to highlight the paintings' colors, and a design system inspired by Envato (a marketplace for web templates and resources) to define buttons, accordion components, tabs, and more.

With the palette and type system set as a small UI kit, the interface was prototyped in Figma Make, combining written prompts, reference images drawn from the sites above, and that UI kit. The resulting prototype can be explored here: Figma Prototype.

Image Assets

The museum interior renders and the archetype portraits photos used throughout this site were generated with Nano Banana (Gemini's image model). The artwork images themselves, the actual colonial paintings corpus, come from the ARCA platform (Universidad de los Andes, Bogotá), the most complete dataset of American colonial art available to date.

WEB DEVELOPMENT

From the Figma Make prototype, the exhibition was implemented as a static, responsive site (no build step or framework as Bootstrap is used) designed to remain legible to both general and academic audiences.

The HTML pages and CSS were built from scratch. For the JavaScript layer, AI assistance was used extensively, particularly to implement the desired animations and interactions. Claude, the AI assistant used, also helped with corrections throughout the process via MCP.
