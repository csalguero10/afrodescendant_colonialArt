ABOUT THE EXHIBITION

This virtual exhibition builds on the physical show to challenge a common misconception: that every person of African descent in colonial Latin America lived in slavery. Through paintings, screens, and portraits, we trace how art from this period both recorded and constructed the racial hierarchies of its time.

Through secular works, religious paintings, and portraits, we discover how Afro-descendant people held diverse and complex roles in colonial society: merchants, artisans, coachmen, wet nurses, confraternity leaders, and even freed entrepreneurs who ran their own businesses.

This show has three goals. First, to show just how varied life could be under the broad label "Afro-descendant" — enslaved, freed, or born free, each with a different lived experience. Second, to unpack casta paintings as a visual system built to sort people into racial categories — and to notice where that system starts to crack. Third, to bring back the individual stories behind the images: real people who shaped the economic, religious, and family life of colonial society, not just subjects to be looked at.
Starting in the 1500s, the transatlantic slave trade brought thousands of Africans to the Americas, giving rise to a large and diverse Afro-descendant population. By the 1700s, casta painting series — like those by Miguel Cabrera or José de Páez — tried to pin down racial identity through image, at a moment when growing mixed-race populations made colonial elites increasingly anxious about maintaining clear social boundaries. Travelers like Thomas Gage marveled at the lavish clothing worn by Afro-descendant women — a style of dress so bold that colonial sumptuary laws tried, and largely failed, to restrict it.

In the Spanish American colonies, Africans and their descendants were often denied individuation in representation and were instead depicted as generic types or as members of a group.1 As Jaime Borja notes in Los ingenios del pincel, "of Black people—mostly enslaved, but also free men who acted as settlers and even conquistadors—no visual memory remains,"2 despite their constant presence in colonial society. These groups remained marginally represented in painting as a reflection of their exclusion from access to the forms of power that guaranteed visibility. In this sense, portraits and colonial painting did not operate merely as representational tools, but as exercises of power, capable of determining who deserved to be seen and how.

Nonetheless, colonial paintings include figures (particularly in the middle and background) whose inclusion itself demonstrates the social fluidity of a world shaped by movement, settlement, and relocation. Historically, figures of Africans and their descendants in Latin American colonial art have received limited interpretive attention. This marginalization extends beyond mere compositional hierarchy, reflecting broader assumptions that naturalized slavery as the expected condition of Black individuals in colonial society. Recent scholarship, however, has begun to challenge these views, highlighting the diverse roles of Black people, from military or commercial positions to artistic production,3 and stressing the need to reconsider their visibility and significance within colonial visual culture.

  3. 3. Juan Correa (1647–1717) and his student José de Ibarra (1685–1756) were two of the most prominent painters in colonial Mexico City, and both were of African descent.
These paintings are more than historical records: they are essential clues for better understanding the sociocultural dynamics that still shape our contemporary society, helping us question stereotypes and recognize the historical diversity of Afro-descendant communities.
